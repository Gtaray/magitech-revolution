Effects and consequences are categorized into five levels of impact: **none**, **limited**, **standard**, **great**, and **extreme**. [[1. How to Play/4. Harm, Stress & Trauma/Harm]] levels, for example, are an easy way to show this concept — limited effect is level 1 harm, standard effect is level 2 harm, great effect is level 3 harm, and extreme effect is level 4 harm (fatal).

### No Effect

If an action has no effect, then it will not change things in a meaningful way. If a crew is facing challenges more 2 or more tiers above their [[3. The Crew/5. Crew Quality\|quality]] then they will have no effect as a baseline, and will need to do something to increase their effect level.

### Limited Effect

Limited effect means an action will have a reduced outcome.

### Standard Effect

The default for a **[[1. How to Play/3. Threat Roll/1. Threat Roll\|threat roll]]**, and will mean that either the threat is resolved or meaningful progress is made to overcome an obstacle.

### Great Effect

You achieve more than would be normally expected.

### Extreme Effect

You have overwhelming advantage over the opposition and are maximally effective against opposition.

# Using Effect

Any kind of effect or consequence can be conceived this way, not just numerical ones. When the 21st Squadron is closing in, how intense is it? Are they still **searching** for the crew (limited), or are they **pursuing** them (standard)? Do they have them **cornered** (great), or are is the crew actually getting **captured** (extreme)?

> _The crew is negotiating with the Ashen Knives, who are higher Tier and more respected, so the threat is great: they might be **unreasonable** in the bargain (see the last row of the examples)._
>
> _If the effect is reduced, the GM could choose **demanding** (standard effect) instead of unreasonable. The Ashen Knives insist on a trade that favors them a bit, but nothing beyond reason._

Opponents who are closely matched usually have standard effect. If a typical PC fights a typical guard, they probably threaten each other with level 2 harm.

In most cases, you can take a bigger risk to have greater effect. The player could say they’re going all-in, fighting their hardest to do harm 3 to the guard, and risking harm 3 in return. They might be able to go the other way — it’s just a mild tussle, so they give and take harm 1.

When opponents aren’t closely matched, the more dangerous one gets more impact for less risk. An average duelist could face lethal harm from a master swordsman, while threatening superficial cuts in return.

When you describe effects and consequences, say what happens in the fiction before adding on any mechanical elements. When everyone can imagine what’s happening, it’s easier to assess the levels of impact and understand the risk/reward factors of the situation.

> _“You can **get a few good cuts in**, but he might **overpower you and put the manacles on**. Let’s say you’re doing harm 2 to him and you might become restrained.”_

# Assessing Factors

To assess effect level, first start with your gut feeling, given this situation. Then, if needed, assess three factors that may modify the effect level: **potency**, **scale**, **and** **quality**. If the PC has an advantage in a given factor, consider a higher effect level. If they have a disadvantage, consider a reduced effect level.

### Potency

This factor considers particular weaknesses, taking extra time or a bigger risk, or the influence of strange powers. A high-end Guild device may be a potent safe-cracker. An magical artifact may be potent against a mystic’s magic. A thief will be more potent **[[2. The Characters/3. Actions/Skulk\|skulking]]** in the dark.

### Quality / Tier

Quality represents the effectiveness of tools, weapons, or other resources, usually summarized by **[[3. The Crew/5. Crew Quality\|crew quality]]** or faction Tier. Fine or masterwork items count as +1 bonus in quality, stacking with Tier/crew quality.

### Scale

Scale represents the number of opponents, size of an area covered, scope of influence, etc. Larger scale can be an advantage or disadvantage depending on the situation. In battle, more people are better. When infiltrating, more people are a hindrance.

When considering factors, effect level might be reduced below limited, resulting in zero effect — or increased beyond great, resulting in an extreme effect.

If a PC special ability gives “+1 effect,” it comes into play after the GM has assessed the effect level. For example, if you ended up with zero effect, the +1 effect bonus from your Pilot’s Exceed Specs ability would bump them up to limited effect.

Also, remember that a PC can push themselves (take 2 stress) to get +1 effect on their action.

## Dominant Factors

If one effect factor overshadows the others, the side with that advantage dominates the situation. It doesn’t matter if you have a fine sword and extra effect if you try to fight 20 people at once. Their scale dominates the battle and you’re left with very limited effect, or no effect at all. The same principle applies to “impossible” actions.

Effect factors are a way to codify the situation into a few key factors so it’s easier to talk about what needs to change in order to have the desired effect.

# Trading Effect for Position

After factors are considered and the GM has announced the effect level, a player might want to trade position for effect, or vice versa. For instance, if they’re going to make a risky roll with standard effect (the most common scenario, generally), they might instead want to push their luck and make a desperate roll but with great effect.

This kind of trade-off isn’t included in the effect factors because it’s not an element the GM should assess when setting the effect level. Once the level is set, though, you can always offer the trade-off to the player if it makes sense in the situation.

> _“I **[[2. The Characters/3. Actions/Skulk]]** across the hanger and vault over the cargo crates, hiding in the shadows by the Guild airship and the exosuits.”_
>
> _“I don’t think you can make it across in one quick dash. The scale of the hanger is a factor here, so your effect will be limited. Let’s say you can get halfway across with this action, then you’ll have to **[[2. The Characters/3. Actions/Skulk]]** through the other half of the space (and the rest of the guards there) to reach the other side.”_
>
> _“I didn’t realize it was that far. Hmmm. Okay, what if I just go as fast as I can. Can I get all the way across if I make a desperate roll?”_
>
> _“Yep, sounds good to me!”_

# Effects in the Fiction

After the **[[1. How to Play/3. Threat Roll/1. Threat Roll\|threat roll]]**, make sure to explain what the achieved effect looks like in the fiction. Leaving it as simply a number of clock segments will keep the story static, whereas explaining the new situation allows the players a way forward into new actions and subsequent rolls. Look at the questions for the effect level as a guide for the kinds of things to explain.

Actions can be simple (resolved entirely in one roll) or complex (resolved by filling up a clock).

There’s no rule for what is a simple action and what is complex. As a guide, think about whether you’d want to see this taken up a few beats, or whether it’s just a tense moment. For the latter, use a simple obstacle, and for the former, use a complex one.
