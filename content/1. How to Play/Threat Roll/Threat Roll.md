When you face a negative **consequence** from a dangerous opponent or challenging situation, make a **threat roll** to see if you can avoid it. Most of the time a threat roll does not determine if a character can do something, but rather how much that something costs. 

To make a threat roll, roll **1d** for each rating in the **action** you're using, plus any **bonus dice** you have.

Take the highest result to determine what happens.

| Result | Outcome                                                                                                     |
| ------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 6      | **Success:** You avoid the threat                                                                           |
| 4-5    | **Peril:** Suffer a reduced consequence or **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Push Yourself\|push yourself]]** to avoid the threat         |
| 1-3    | **Threat:** You suffer the consequence. If you **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Push Yourself\|push yourself]]**, reduce the consequence |
If you're facing multiple threats at once, add **1d** to your pool for every additional threat, then assign a die result to each threat.

> *Oskarr is binding a wild fire spirit to a totem. The GM describes two threats:*
> 
> *A **wild flare** from the spirit (harm 2) and **Earthen Enclave attention** (two ticks on their Alert clock).*
> 
> *Oskarr’s player rolls four dice: three for Attune and one more for the additional threat—and gets 6, 4, 4, 2. He assigns the 6 to the wild flare threat, so he avoids it. He assigns a 4 to the Earthen Enclave threat, so he suffers a reduced consequence (one tick on the clock) or may push himself to avoid it entirely.*

## Establishing the Threat
To initiate a threat roll, the GM describes a bad **consequence** that's about to happen and you narrate your character's response.

> *“Mylera suddenly draws her blade in the blink of an eye! **She might cut you down before you can react.**”*
> 
> *“I lunge forward as she draws and try to wrestle her to the floor before she can get her sword out.”*

If your character's action initiates the threat, the GM first describes your **effect** and then defines the possible bad **consequence** so you know the risk/reward before you roll.

> *“You **sneak into the manor** through the skylight, but you might **draw the attention of the guards** (two ticks on the Alert clock).”*
> 
> *“You **search the office to find the hidden files,** but the servants might **catch you in the act.**”*
> 
> *“You **disable the complicated magic alarm,** but you might **break your hexcracking tools** in the process.”*
> 
> *“You pummel the guard and **knock him out,** but during the fight, you might **take a bad hit,** too (harm 2).*

## Choosing the Action
Based on your description of your scoundrel’s response to the threat, the GM proposes an **[[1. How to Play/Actions & Attributes/Actions\|action]]** to use for the roll.

If the proposed action doesn’t fit your idea of what your character is doing, describe the scene in more detail to reflect the action rating you intend. If the group can’t agree for some reason, the player has the final call on which action rating to roll.

In some special cases, you can use other ratings when you make a threat roll, like an **[[1. How to Play/Actions & Attributes/Attributes\|attribute]]**, **[[3. The Crew/Crew Quality\|crew quality]]**, or **[[2. The Characters/Rules/Stash#Lifestyle\|lifestyle]]**.

## Set Position And Effect
Once the player chooses their action, the GM sets the **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Position]]** and **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Effect]]** for the roll. **Position** represents how dangerous the threat is. **Effect** is how effective the action will be at overcoming the threat.

- Position can be **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Position#Controlled\|controlled]]**, **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Position#Risky\|risky]]**, or **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Position#Desperate\|desperate]]**.
- Effect can be **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Effect#No Effect\|none]]**, **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Effect#Limited Effect\|limited]]**, **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Effect#Standard Effect\|standard]]**, **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Effect#Great Effect\|great]]**, or **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Effect#Extreme Effect\|extreme]]**.

By default, a threat roll is **risky** with **standard** effect.

## Additional Threats
The GM can add additional threats depending on the situation (and the players may suggest them), including threats from the **environment**, NPC **strengths**, or **magical** effects.

Additional threats essentially act as bonus dice to your roll. You have to assign a result to them (so you’re in more danger), but by adding more dice, you’re more likely to avoid any single threat.

If you’re facing a really bad consequence, suggest possible additional threats to the GM to increase your dice pool.

> *“Ok, I’m just blasting away, firing and reloading as fast as possible... can I add the threat that I **run out of ammo?**”*
> 
> *“I really don’t want to get captured by the guard... can I add the threat that I **suffer harm**—by jumping out the window?*

## Gambits
Before making the **threat roll**, a player can choose to spend one of their crew's **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Gambits]]** to add **1d** to their roll.

## Special Cases
Most of the time, a standard threat roll is all you need to resolve a dangerous situation. However, there are some special cases that work better with a few tweaks to the mechanic, so they’re presented here as options to use when you need them.

### Controlled Position
If a PC is ever in a position where there’s no impending danger, don’t call for a threat roll — just describe what they’re able to accomplish without taking a risk or paying a cost. If they want to achieve more, they can make a **[[1. How to Play/Devil's Bargain]]** to do it.

### Desperate Position
If your PC is outnumbered, outclassed, or badly impaired (perhaps due to harm), they need to make a **desperate** roll to avoid the threat. A desperate roll is all-or-nothing: a 6 is a success, but a 5
or less is a threat result — you suffer the consequence.

| **Result** | **Outcome**                                                                                                  |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **6**      | **Success:** You avoid the threat.                                                                           |
| **1-5**    | **Threat:** You suffer the consequence. If you **[[1. How to Play/Threat Roll/Push Yourself\|push yourself]]**, reduce the consequences |
When you make a desperate threat roll, [[2. The Characters/Character Advancement\|mark xp]]. 

The GM can call for a desperate roll to indicate that a threat is more serious, beyond the usual dangers a scoundrel faces. Fighting a gang enforcer is risky, but if you’re jumped by three of them at once, things are desperate. Sneaking past the bouncer at the Leaky Bucket is risky, but the security at the Centuralia Club is in a whole different league.

Fictional positioning and character abilities can mitigate desperate threats. If a PC has a rifle and time to fire and reload before the three enforcers can reach them, or if they’re **[[2. The Characters/Playbooks/Muscle#Unstoppable\|Unstoppable]]**, for example, facing three enemies at once wouldn’t be desperate — it’s a standard threat roll in that case. 

When you call for a desperate roll, ask the player if they want to carry on with their course of action or try a different way (if possible). A clever flashback, cunning maneuver, or help from a teammate might be enough to improve their position.

### Using An Attribute
Sometimes it makes sense to use a PC’s **[[1. How to Play/Actions & Attributes/Attributes\|attribute rating]]** for a threat roll. This works well when a character is trying to avoid a threat resulting from their own potential weaknesses.

- Roll **[[1. How to Play/Actions & Attributes/Attributes#Insight\|Insight]]** against threats involving disorientation, confusion, doubt, or uncertainty.
- Roll **[[1. How to Play/Actions & Attributes/Attributes#Prowess\|Prowess]]** against threats resulting from pushing physical limits (strength, speed, endurance), exhaustion, or deprivations.
- Roll **[[1. How to Play/Actions & Attributes/Attributes#Resolve\|Resolve]]** against threats involving temptation, lack of self-control, willpower, hesitation, or loss of nerve.

> *“When Bazso stares me down, I hesitate. I might be too intimidated to stand up to him. I want to roll Resolve and see what happens.”*

## Using Other Ratings
You can use other ratings with a threat roll as well, like the crew's **[[3. The Crew/Crew Quality\|Quality]]** when their clout or reputation is threatened, or a PC’s **[[2. The Characters/Rules/Stash#Lifestyle Rating\|lifestyle rating]]** when their affluence or apparent wealth is key to avoiding a threat. Feel free to get creative when the situation calls for it.

### The Threat of Failure
A threat roll usually resolves the consequence of a threat; it doesn’t bear on the effect of your scoundrel’s own action. However, sometimes the success of an action seems unlikely or especially difficult. In this case, the GM can introduce the threat of **failure**.

If you assign a 4/5 to a failure threat, your character’s action has reduced effect or otherwise doesn’t go as you hoped. On a 1-3, the action fails. You can push yourself to improve the outcome, as usual.

> *“This is a long sniper shot, so the threat is failure. **You might miss!** And you’re not a trained sharpshooter, so it’s a desperate roll, too.”*
>
> *“Damn, I got a 4. That means I miss, right? Okay, if I push myself to reduce the consequence, what happens?”*
> 
> *“Let’s say that you manage to land a shot, but not as well as you hoped — you hit him in the arm, not the chest.”*
